Challenges of Introducing Technology in Indian Agriculture

By Jagriti Shahi · 12 Feb 2025

Challenges of Introducing Technology in Indian Agriculture

Here are some key facts related to the challenges of introducing technology in Indian agriculture:

High Cost of Technology

  • The cost of basic agricultural drones in India ranges from INR 4 lakhs to INR 15 lakhs (USD 5,000 - 20,000). For small-scale farmers with limited capital, such high costs are unaffordable (Source: Economic Times).

Electricity and Infrastructure

  • As of 2021, 24% of rural Indian households reported issues with inconsistent or unreliable electricity, making it difficult for farmers to use technology-dependent systems like automated irrigation or sensor-based monitoring (Source: Ministry of Power).

Traditional Practices

  • More than 60% of Indian farmers still rely on age-old farming techniques, which often conflict with the modern technologies being introduced. The challenge is to balance traditional knowledge with technology to improve productivity (Source: India Rural Development Report 2018).

Training and Extension Services

  • According to a 2019 NITI Aayog report, only 32% of Indian farmers have access to any form of agricultural extension services, which are critical for training and educating farmers about new technologies.

Water Usage

  • Agriculture accounts for over 80% of India's freshwater usage. However, only 5% of Indian farmers use modern irrigation systems like drip or sprinkler irrigation, which are more efficient than traditional flood irrigation methods (Source: World Bank, Ministry of Agriculture).

Internet Access in Rural India

The gradual increase in rural internet penetration is a positive indicator of digital connectivity reaching underserved areas. Despite this progress, rural internet usage still lags behind urban areas by nearly 30%, highlighting a critical barrier to adopting digital agricultural technologies such as mobile apps for farming advice or precision agriculture. Bridging this gap is essential to ensure equitable access to agricultural innovations in India.

Marginal and small farmers make up over 85% of the farming community in India, with most cultivating less than 2 hectares of land. These small farm sizes present challenges in adopting expensive modern agricultural technologies, such as precision farming tools and drones, which are often costly and require larger fields to be effective. Economies of scale are limited in small farms, making it harder for these farmers to invest in advanced technologies without financial support.

Adoption of Agricultural Technology

The adoption of advanced agricultural technologies in India remains low, with fewer than 6% of farmers using automated irrigation systems and less than 2% utilizing precision farming tools or drones. This low adoption rate underscores the barriers farmers face, such as high upfront costs, lack of technical knowledge, and limited access to financing. However, a relatively higher percentage of farmers (15.6%) are using mobile apps for advice, indicating a potential entry point for further technological interventions.

Post-Harvest Losses in India by Commodity

Post-harvest losses are a major challenge in Indian agriculture, particularly for fruits and vegetables, where almost 16% of produce is lost after harvest. These losses contribute significantly to food insecurity and waste. Technologies that enhance storage, processing, and logistics are critical to reducing these losses and improving the income of farmers. Investments in cold chain infrastructure and innovations like smart packaging could help address this issue.

Financial Inclusion in Rural India

Despite gradual improvements in financial inclusion, with over half of farmers now having access to institutional credit, a large number still rely on informal sources like moneylenders, which often come with high interest rates. Access to affordable credit is a critical factor in enabling farmers to adopt new technologies, buy better equipment, and improve productivity. Digital financial services and government-backed credit schemes have helped improve this access, but further efforts are needed.

Mobile Phone Ownership in Rural India - 2023

The increasing penetration of mobile phones in rural India is a significant enabler for digital transformation in agriculture. With nearly 94% of rural households having access to mobile phones, there is immense potential for the widespread use of mobile-based agricultural services. Apps providing weather updates, farming advice, and market prices could greatly benefit rural farmers, helping them make informed decisions and improve their productivity.

Labor costs for activities like manual irrigation and harvesting continue to rise, with daily wages ranging from INR 300 to 600 depending on the region and type of work. Mechanization, such as using automated irrigation systems or drones for crop monitoring, could reduce dependency on manual labor and increase operational efficiency, but these technologies must become more affordable for small-scale farmers.

The cost of building agricultural infrastructure such as cold storage facilities or warehousing is substantial but more affordable for small-scale operations, particularly with government subsidies. For instance, a small cold storage unit for fruits and vegetables can range between INR 8 to 20 lakhs, which is feasible for larger cooperatives but still expensive for individual farmers. Improving access to micro-loans or subsidized schemes can make these technologies more attainable.

Several key developments have emerged in the context of introducing technology into Indian agriculture:

  • Digital Literacy Initiatives: The Pradhan Mantri Gramin Digital Saksharta Abhiyan (PMGDISHA) has significantly advanced digital literacy in rural India. By March 31, 2024, approximately 6.39 crore (63.9 million) rural residents were trained under this scheme, surpassing the initial target of 6 crore. Notably, Uttar Pradesh led with 1.45 crore beneficiaries, followed by Bihar with 74.12 lakh. government.economictimes.indiatimes.com
  • Small and Marginal Farmers: India's agricultural landscape remains predominantly composed of small and marginal farmers. A survey conducted by the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) between July 2022 and June 2023 revealed that among individuals aged 15-24 years, approximately 78.4% possessed the skills to send messages, emails, and attachments. Additionally, about 94.2% of rural households and 97.1% of urban households owned telephones and/or mobile phones. telecom.economictimes.indiatimes.com
  • Financial Challenges: The agricultural sector continues to face financial hurdles. In July 2024, reports indicated that top private lenders in India were experiencing an increase in farm loan defaults. Farmers and rural borrowers struggled with delayed repayments due to unpredictable weather conditions and rising agricultural input costs. The largest five private banks reported higher bad loan additions in the June quarter, notably in agriculture and microfinance loans. reuters.com
  • Climate Resilience and Technological Adoption: Climate change poses significant challenges to Indian agriculture. In Rayanpet village, farmers have faced unpredictable rains and increasing heat, jeopardizing their rice crops. To address these issues, agricultural research organizations have developed climate-resilient rice varieties that yield more with less water and offer higher disease resistance. Over the past decade, more than 2,000 resilient seed varieties have been released, with plans to cover 25% of paddy fields with these seeds in the upcoming winter season. apnews.com
